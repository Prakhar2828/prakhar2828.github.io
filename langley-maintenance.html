Work from my NASA Langley internship · November 2026

Predictive maintenance
at Langley.

What I learned from bearing vibration, a train compressor, and the tests that made the results less straightforward.

At Langley, I built a hands-on predictive-maintenance study and workshop using two public datasets: CWRU bearing recordings and MetroPT-3 compressor telemetry. I wanted to understand what the measurements could tell us, and what someone maintaining the equipment could reasonably do with that information.

The work involved checking the signals, extracting features, comparing machine learning with simpler physical rules, and testing where the results stopped holding up. That last part became one of the most useful things I learned.

About what’s shared here

This is a summary of my methods and results. The code, notebooks and working files are not public yet. These experiments use public benchmark data, not measurements from NASA facilities. They were research and workshop work, not a deployed maintenance system.

Before the model

I worked with 40 CWRU recordings and 1,516,948 historical MetroPT-3 readings. I checked file hashes, signal orientation, sample rates, timestamps and the physical meaning of the channels. For the bearing signals, I brought the healthy recordings from 48 kHz to 12 kHz with anti-alias filtering before comparing them with the fault recordings.

I used FFT, Welch power spectra, STFT and envelope analysis to examine the vibration, then extracted features for classification. The compressor analysis combined pressure, current and operating-state information with anomaly scoring.

After the model

I kept source recordings and load conditions separated in the bearing tests. For the compressor, I used early-February training, later-February calibration and March-onward retrospective testing. I compared alert policies, grouped repeated flags into review episodes, and checked apparent warnings against time-shifted alert schedules.

I also built review cards that connect a measured change to possible checks, uncertainties and the maintenance owner’s decision. A flag was the beginning of an investigation, not an instruction to replace a part.

CWRU · Seeded laboratory bearing faults

The harder test mattered.

The first classifier result looked strong: 97.2% window-level balanced accuracy when I trained a Random Forest on 0–2 HP recordings and tested on 3 HP. The ten held-out recordings were all classified correctly when their window predictions were combined by majority vote.

Then I held out a defect diameter as well as the load. On the unseen 0.021-inch defect condition, balanced accuracy was 52.8%. That is a very different picture of how well the model generalizes.

CWRU’s faults were deliberately seeded into laboratory bearings. These recordings are not a run-to-failure history, and files with different operating conditions may still come from the same physical bearing. The first result cannot establish performance on a new machine or naturally developing damage. Showing the second result alongside it matters to me.

MetroPT-3 · Historical train-compressor telemetry

Would the alerts actually help
someone reviewing the equipment?

The compressor data had a real time axis and four reported event periods. I compared an Isolation Forest with a simple pressure rule and a daily physical rule. Instead of counting every flagged interval separately, I grouped the flags into candidate review episodes.

The pressure rule produced 93 episodes, compared with 387 from Isolation Forest: about 76% fewer candidate reviews in this retrospective comparison. That is a workload comparison, not a measured reduction in staff hours or maintenance costs.

Why I don’t call this proven failure prediction

I shifted the alert schedules in time and checked them again. 180 of 184 shifted Isolation Forest schedules still matched all four event periods. For the pressure rule, 18 of 184 did.

When so many shifted schedules also look successful, matching four events in the original schedule is not enough evidence that the model predicted them. Four historical events are a small basis for any reliability claim.

Connecting measurements to a review

What I would ask someone
to check next.

I also examined changes that could be explained in physical terms. Compared with the early-February reference, the later-period medians showed:

Pressure loss while stopped

0.048 → 0.084bar per minute

Check pressure sensing, comparable operating periods and possible leakage.

Running-current fraction

29.1% → 47.2%share of observations

Check demand, schedules and loading before treating increased duty as a fault.

Motor starts

40.5 → 57starts per day

Check cycling behavior and whether the operating conditions have changed.

Those are observations, not confirmed leaks, repairs or root causes. The review cards I built paired measured changes with corroborating checks and uncertainty. A maintenance owner would still need to review the evidence and decide what action was justified.

What I’m taking from this

A useful result can include
finding out where the model falls short.

This work made me pay more attention to the question behind an evaluation. Can the model recognize a seeded fault under another load? Can it handle a different defect size? Is an alert early enough to be useful, or is there so much alerting that matching an event is easy?

Those questions matter more to me than presenting one attractive accuracy number. If I want my work to help someone responsible for equipment, I need to explain what it supports, what remains uncertain, and what they should check before acting.

I did not demonstrate prevented failures, savings at NASA, prospective warning performance or deployment on Langley equipment. The contribution here is the analysis, the comparisons, the checks, and the connection to a human maintenance review.
